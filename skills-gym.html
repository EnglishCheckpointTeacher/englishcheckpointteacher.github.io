Workout 1 · In every real paper

Punctuation detective

Every Checkpoint paper asks why a writer used a punctuation mark. You don’t just name the mark: you say what job it does in that sentence.

Method: read the words after the mark. Is it a list? An explanation? Extra information? A new sentence that links closely? The words after the mark usually tell you its job.
Workout 2 · In every real paper

Word hunter

“Give one word from paragraph 3 that means…” is quick to answer, but only if you search in the right place and copy the word exactly.

Method: the answer is always the same word class as the clue. If the clue is an adjective (confused), look for an adjective. Try swapping your word into the clue’s place: does the sentence still make sense?
Workout 3 · Cohesion

Link it back

Writers use words like this, these, it and as a result to tie sentences and paragraphs together. Real papers ask what those words point back to.

Method: read the sentence before. Ask “this what?” or “these what?”, then find the noun or idea that fits. Test it by replacing the word with your answer.
Workout 4 · Language

Technique spotter

Name the technique quickly, then you can spend your time on what really earns marks: its effect.

Method: like or as → simile. Is something else → metaphor. A thing acting like a person → personification. Repeated sounds → alliteration or sibilance. A sound word → onomatopoeia. Always add: this suggests…
Workout 5 · Grammar for effect

Sentence effects

Questions about sentence structure appear in almost every paper: short sentences, long sentences, clauses placed first, one-line paragraphs.

Method: short = tension, shock or emphasis. Long = flowing description, or lots happening at once. Something placed first is being emphasised. A paragraph on its own = a turning point or a dramatic pause.
Workout 6 · Structure

Put it in order

Real papers ask you to number information in the order it appears, or to match paragraphs to their ideas. Practise seeing how texts are built.

Method: look for linking words (these, as a result, however, meanwhile). A sentence that starts with these or this can never come first. Use the ↑ and ↓ buttons to move each line.
Workout 7 · Worth 4 marks in nearly every paper

The 4-mark question

“Do you think…? Tick yes or no. Give two quotations and explain how each one supports your answer.” This is usually the biggest reading question, so it is worth getting right.

Method: there is no wrong side, only unsupported answers. Pick the side you can prove best. Choose short quotations that really prove your point, then explain how in your own words. Never just repeat the quotation.
Workout 8 · Paper 1

Summary squeezer

Paper 1 often asks for a 40 or 50-word summary. The marks go to the right points written in joined-up sentences. Notes or a list can score 0, and going over the limit caps you at 1 mark.

Method: underline only what answers the question. Cross out examples, numbers, quotations, problems (if the question asks for benefits) and opinions. Then join the points in your own words and count.
