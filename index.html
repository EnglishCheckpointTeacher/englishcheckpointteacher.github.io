How to use this site

Three steps to exam day

Each exam page follows the same order, so you always know what to do next.

  1. Know the paper

    Start with the papers section: how long you have, what each section asks, and how to share your time between reading and writing.

  2. Learn the methods

    Every question type has a method, and every kind of writing has a checklist and a model answer. Learn them until they are automatic.

  3. Practise and check

    Take the quiz, listen to the tips, tick off the revision checklist, and come back to the parts you found hard.

First Language or Second Language?

Your school decides which exam you are entered for. This is how the two are different.

English (First Language)English as a Second Language
Who it is forLearners who use English as their main language at school, at home or bothLearners who are developing English alongside another main language
PapersTwo papers: Non-fiction and Fiction. Each has a reading section and a writing sectionThree papers: Reading and Usage, Listening, and Writing
ReadingLonger fiction and non-fiction texts. Explain meaning, language choices, structure and the writer's purposeShorter texts. Find information, understand main ideas and opinions, and choose the right word
WritingExtended writing for a purpose and audience: stories, descriptions, reports, arguments, lettersShorter, practical writing such as emails, messages and short stories, with accurate grammar
GrammarTested inside the reading and writing tasksTested directly in "usage" questions
ListeningNot testedTested in its own paper. You hear each recording twice
Good to know

How Checkpoint works

The same rules apply to all four English exams.

When it happens

Checkpoint tests run in three series each year: March, May and October. Your school chooses the series and tells you the exact dates.

Who marks it

You sit the papers at your own school, but they are marked by Cambridge examiners, not by your teacher.

What you get back

A statement of achievement and a report showing how you did overall and in each skill, so you and your teacher can see your strengths.

Questions and answers

For students and parents

What is Cambridge Checkpoint?

Checkpoint is a set of tests from Cambridge for the end of the primary programme (Stage 6) and the end of the lower secondary programme (Stage 9). It shows what a learner has understood in English, mathematics and science, and gives feedback on strengths and areas to improve before the next stage of school.

Can you pass or fail Checkpoint?

No. Checkpoint is not a pass or fail exam. It is a check on your progress. The report shows how well you did in each skill, so the result is most useful as a guide to what to work on next.

How long before the exam should I start revising?

About four weeks of steady revision works well for most learners. Each exam page has a four-week plan and a checklist you can tick off. Twenty focused minutes a day is better than one long session at the weekend.

Where can I find past papers?

Cambridge gives past papers and specimen papers to registered schools through its School Support Hub, so ask your teacher. This site teaches the skills and methods you need for those papers, with original practice questions. For Lower Secondary English, see Past papers explained to find out what recent papers ask, then try the two mock papers.

Does this site work without the internet?

You need a connection to open each page. Once a page is open you can keep reading it, and you can print any exam page to revise on paper.

Is this an official Cambridge website?

No. English Checkpoint is an independent site written by a teacher. For official information, follow the links to Cambridge at the bottom of each exam page, and always check exam dates and details with your school.

Ready to test yourself?

Every exam page has a bank of twenty questions. They come in a new order each time, with an explanation for every answer.
