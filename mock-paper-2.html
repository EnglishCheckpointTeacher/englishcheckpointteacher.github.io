The Lantern Race

Every autumn, the children of a riverside village race paper lanterns down the river. Mira has made her lantern with her grandfather, whose eyesight is failing.

The river was the colour of strong tea, and it was in a hurry. Mira could hear it from the top of the bank: a low, endless grumble, as if it had somewhere better to be.

‘You’re late,’ said Joss. Her cousin was already crouched at the water’s edge, guarding a lantern shaped like a dragon. It had glittering scales, a curling tail and – Mira counted twice – four battery-powered lights inside it. ‘Dad bought the kit online,’ he added, before she could ask.

Mira said nothing. She held her own lantern against her chest the way you might hold a small bird. It was a plain paper boat, its sides painted with blue waves that had smudged a little in the rain. Inside, a single candle stub waited to be lit.

Three weeks earlier, she had sat at Grandad’s kitchen table while the radio muttered in the corner. His fingers, which had once mended fishing nets faster than anyone in the village, moved slowly now. He could no longer see the fine lines on a ruler, so Mira measured and he folded, and between them the boat had grown, crease by careful crease.

‘Paper remembers,’ he had told her, pressing a fold flat with his thumbnail. ‘Fold it properly once and it’ll hold its shape for ever.’

‘Will it float?’ she had asked.

He had laughed – a dry, rattling laugh, like pebbles in a tin. ‘It’ll float if you believe in it. And if you remember the wax.’

She had remembered the wax. She had rubbed every inch of the hull with an old candle until her fingers ached and the paper shone like the skin of an apple.

A horn sounded. All along the bank, children lowered their lanterns onto the water: stars, swans, fish, a wobbly castle, a lantern shaped like a pineapple. The current seized them at once. Joss’s dragon surged ahead, its four lights blazing, its tail swinging proudly from side to side.

Mira knelt. Her hands were shaking so much that it took three matches to light the candle. Then she let go.

For a moment, the little boat simply sat there, turning in a slow circle as if it could not decide which way to go. Somebody laughed. Mira felt her face grow hot.

Then the river found it.

It slid out into the middle of the stream, small and steady, its candle flame bending but never going out. Behind it, the pineapple collapsed. The castle tipped, filled with water and sank without a sound. Up ahead, the dragon’s tail – so splendid on the bank – caught the current like a sail and spun the whole lantern sideways into the reeds. Its four lights glowed on, uselessly, among the mud.

Joss stared. ‘That’s not fair,’ he muttered. ‘Mine cost forty pounds.’

Mira did not answer. She was running along the bank now, her trainers slapping through puddles, her eyes fixed on one tiny point of light. Past the old mill. Past the footbridge. Past the crowd of grown-ups with their phones held high. By the time it reached the finishing rope, the paper boat was the only lantern left on the water.

Later, when the prizes had been given out and the bank was emptying, Mira walked home slowly through the damp streets. Grandad’s window was lit. She could see him in his chair by the radio, waiting.

She would tell him everything, she decided: the matches, the laugh, the dragon in the reeds. But first she would tell him the most important part.

Paper remembers.

Question 12 · 25 marks

Mira and her grandfather make a lantern together. Write a story called The Gift about a character who makes something by hand for someone important to them.

  • who your character is, and what they make
  • a problem they have to overcome
  • how the story ends.

Step 1 · Decode the task

Which plan will make the best story in 25 minutes?

Keep it small. One event in a short time span gives you room to describe feelings and details, which is where the marks are.

Which opening hooks the reader best?

It starts in the middle of the action, with a problem, and uses strong verbs. The reader wants to know what happens next.

How should you show that your character is nervous?

Show, don’t tell. An action lets the reader work out the feeling, just as the writer does with Mira in paragraph 10.

    Model story: The Gift

    The glue gun spat, hissed, and died.1

    Zain stared at it. Then he stared at the clock above the cooker: 11:47 p.m. In nine hours and thirteen minutes, his little sister would turn seven,2 and the dolls’ house he had promised her was still a cardboard box with a door-shaped hole in it.

    He had planned it for weeks.3 Lina wanted a house ‘with a staircase that goes round and round, and a tiny light, and a cat’. She had said it so seriously, counting each wish on her fingers, that he had nodded as if it were a contract.

    Now the kitchen table looked like a battlefield. Lolly sticks lay scattered like fallen soldiers; scraps of wallpaper curled at the edges; a jar of buttons had tipped over and rolled into every corner.4

    Zain took a breath. No glue gun. Fine. He found Mum’s sewing box, threaded a needle with clumsy fingers and began to stitch the staircase together, one lolly stick at a time. It was slow – painfully slow – but each step held.5

    By two o’clock,6 the staircase spiralled up through the middle of the house. By three, a torn sock had become a sleeping cat on the bottom stair. The light was the hardest part: a bicycle lamp taped inside the roof, its switch hidden behind a paper chimney.

    He must have fallen asleep, because the next thing he knew, a small hand was shaking his shoulder. ‘Zain. Zain. Is that… mine?’

    Lina was standing in her pyjamas, her hair sticking up on one side. She did not say anything else. She just reached out and pressed the chimney, and the little house filled with light.

    The staircase was wonky. The cat had one eye. It was, without question, the most beautiful thing he had ever made.7

    Why it earns marks

    1. A one-line opening that starts with a problem. Three verbs build to “died”.
    2. An exact time creates a deadline and builds tension.
    3. A short flashback explains why the gift matters.
    4. A simile, and semicolons that separate a list of clauses.
    5. Dashes add emphasis. Short sentences show determination.
    6. Time phrases move the story on smoothly.
    7. Short sentences list the flaws, then a longer one shows his feelings: show, don’t tell.
