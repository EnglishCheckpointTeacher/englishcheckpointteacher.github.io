The Wall Builders of the Dales

Why an old craft that uses no cement at all is quietly making a comeback.

On a hillside above the village of Kettlewell, Ruth Atkinson is reading stones. She turns each one over in her calloused hands, weighs it, frowns, and sets it down again. ‘Every stone has one place where it wants to be,’ she says, without looking up. ‘My job is to find it.’

Ruth is a dry-stone waller, one of a small band of craftspeople who build walls without a single drop of mortar or cement. Instead, the walls stand up because of gravity, friction and an extraordinary amount of patience. Across the north of England, these grey ribbons of stone stretch for thousands of miles, climbing slopes so steep that it is hard to imagine anyone carrying a bucket up them, let alone a tonne of rock.

Many of the walls were built more than two hundred years ago, when open hillsides were divided into fields. Teams of wallers lived in rough huts on the moors for months at a time; they were paid by the length of wall they finished, so speed mattered as much as skill. Some of their work has outlived the farms it was built to protect.

How does a wall with no glue survive two hundred years of storms? The secret lies in its structure. A dry-stone wall is really two walls leaning gently against each other, so it is wider at the bottom than at the top – a shape that wallers call the ‘batter’. Long ‘through stones’ are laid across both sides to tie them together, and the gap in the middle is packed tightly with small stones known as ‘hearting’. Finally, a row of upright ‘copestones’ along the top locks everything in place.

‘People think the big stones do all the work,’ Ruth explains. ‘They don’t. It’s the hearting. Leave a hole in the middle and in ten years you’ll have a heap.’ She says this cheerfully; mistakes, she believes, are the best teachers.

A skilled waller might rebuild only a few metres of wall in a day. It is slow, back-breaking work, carried out in wind, drizzle and, now and then, glorious sunshine. Yet Ruth would not swap it for anything. ‘I’ve worked in an office,’ she says. ‘I lasted eleven months. Out here, at the end of the day, I can stand back and see exactly what I’ve done.’

That sense of achievement is drawing a new generation to the craft. Training courses that once struggled to attract students are now full, and many of the learners are young people looking for a career that keeps them outdoors. Others are retired engineers, teachers and even a former surgeon, who told Ruth that rebuilding a wall felt ‘rather like putting a skeleton back together’.

The walls matter for more than tradition. They shelter sheep from bitter winter winds and provide homes for wildlife: lizards bask on the warm stones, insects hide in the cracks, and small birds such as wrens nest in the gaps. Unlike a wire fence, a stone wall never needs to be thrown away. When it falls, the same stones can be used again – and again.

Not everyone is convinced. Some farmers argue that walls are too expensive to repair and that fences do the job just as well. Repairing a long stretch of collapsed wall can cost far more than putting up a fence, and the work cannot be rushed.

Ruth understands the argument, but she does not accept it. ‘A fence lasts twenty years if you’re lucky,’ she says, lifting another stone and turning it slowly in the light. ‘This wall was here before my great-grandmother was born. If I do my job properly, it’ll still be here when my great-grandchildren are old.’ She pauses. Then the stone drops into place with a soft, satisfying clunk – exactly where it wanted to be.

Question 12 · 25 marks

A local radio station is making a programme called Made to Last. Write an article for its website about a skill, craft or tradition in your area that you think should be kept alive.

  • what the skill or tradition is, and who does it
  • why it matters to you and to others
  • what might be lost if it disappears.

Step 1 · Decode the task

What text type must you write?

An article: so use a headline, a hook, topic sentences and a confident voice. Writing the wrong text type limits your content mark.

Who is your audience?

The general public, so write in a friendly but fairly formal register. No text-speak.

What is your main purpose?

Inform readers about the tradition, and persuade them it should be kept alive.

    A model answer, with notes

    Model article: Threads of the Desert: Why Sadu Weaving Must Not Disappear

    Have you ever looked closely at a cushion in your grandmother’s majlis?1 Those bold red and black stripes, those tiny white triangles marching along the edge, are not just decoration. They are a language – and it is one that very few people can still speak.

    Sadu is a traditional form of weaving made by Bedouin women.2 For centuries, they spun wool from sheep, camels and goats, dyed it with colours from plants, and wove it on a simple loom pegged to the ground. The results were tents, rugs and saddle bags tough enough to survive the desert, yet beautiful enough to be treasured for generations.

    To me, Sadu is personal. My great-aunt learned to weave when she was nine. When she works, her fingers move so quickly that the patterns seem to grow by themselves, and every design has a meaning: a row of diamonds might stand for the eyes of a camel; a zigzag, the shape of the dunes.3

    However, the skill is fading.4 Machines can copy the patterns in minutes, and cheap factory rugs fill the shops. Young people are busy with school and screens, and many have never even seen a loom. If nobody learns, the meanings hidden in those patterns will vanish with the last weavers.

    Fortunately, it is not too late. Our school could easily invite a weaver to teach an after-school club.5 Imagine a display of student work at the next open day: proof that an ancient craft can have a modern future.

    So next time you sit on that old cushion, look down. Somebody’s patience is beneath you.6 The least we can do is make sure that patience is passed on.

    Why it earns marks

    1. A question speaks straight to the reader, and the metaphor “a language” makes the craft sound precious.
    2. A clear topic sentence, then facts that inform the reader.
    3. Personal detail, and a colon and semicolon used accurately to list meanings.
    4. “However” turns the article towards the problem.
    5. A practical solution aimed at the audience.
    6. A circular ending that returns to the cushion, with a short sentence for impact.
