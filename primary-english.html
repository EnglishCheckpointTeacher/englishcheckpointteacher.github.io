Primary Checkpoint English

For learners at the end of Stage 6 who use English as their main language. Two papers, each with a reading section and a writing task.

The papers

Both papers follow the same pattern: read a text and answer questions about it, then write your own text of the same kind.

Paper 1

Non-fiction

1 hour50 marks

Section A · Reading · 25 marks

  • One or two non-fiction texts, such as an article, report, leaflet, review, diary or biography
  • Find information, explain what words mean and comment on the writer's choices
  • Some questions on grammar, punctuation and how the text is organised

Section B · Writing · 25 marks

  • One non-fiction task linked to the reading topic: a report, letter, article, set of instructions or balanced argument
Paper 2

Fiction

1 hour50 marks

Section A · Reading · 25 marks

  • An extract from a story. Sometimes a poem or a scene from a play
  • Questions on character, setting, mood and what happens
  • How the writer uses words and sentences to create effects

Section B · Writing · 25 marks

  • Write your own story, or part of a story, from a prompt

Where the marks are

How it's marked

Cambridge examiners mark your papers. Knowing what they reward helps you spend your effort in the right places.

Reading skills

Each reading question tests one of these skills.

  • Finding information that is stated clearly in the text
  • Reading between the lines to work out what is suggested, not stated
  • Words and language: what words mean and why the writer chose them
  • Structure: how the text is organised and why
  • Grammar and punctuation and the effect they have
  • Your response: purpose, audience, viewpoint and text type

Writing marks

Your writing gets a mark for each of five things. Typical marks out of 25:

  • Structure of your text7Paragraphs, a clear order, linking words, a proper opening and ending
  • Grammar and punctuation7Correct sentences of different kinds, accurate punctuation
  • Content, purpose and audience5Does it do the job the task asked for? Is it interesting?
  • Vocabulary and language3Precise, well-chosen words
  • Spelling3Including harder words, not only easy ones

What this means for you: more than half of the writing marks are for structure, grammar and punctuation. Clear paragraphs and accurate sentences earn more than a long, exciting story with no full stops.

Reading section

Question types and how to answer them

Most reading questions are one of these types. Spot the type, then use the method.

Find and copy

"Find and copy a word that shows…"

Usually 1 mark

The answer is in the text. Copy it exactly and keep it short. If the question asks for one word, write one word only. Extra words can lose the mark.

Retrieve information

"Give two reasons why…"

1 to 2 marks

Find the paragraph the question points to. Give exactly the number of points asked for, one per line. You can use the words of the text.

Tick, circle or match

"Tick two boxes that are true."

1 mark

Check how many answers are needed. If you tick three boxes when it asks for two, you get no mark. Cross out clearly if you change your mind.

Vocabulary in context

"What does 'reluctant' mean in this sentence?"

1 mark

Read the whole sentence again. Try your meaning in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, you are right.

Inference

"How do you know Sam is nervous?"

1 to 2 marks

The answer is not written directly. Give your idea, then back it up with a short quotation: Sam is nervous because his hands are "shaking".

Language effect

"Why has the writer used the word 'crept'?"

1 to 2 marks

Say what the word makes you imagine or feel. "Crept" suggests moving slowly and quietly, so it makes the character seem secretive. Never write only "it makes it interesting".

Figurative language

"What technique is 'the wind howled'?"

1 to 2 marks

Name the technique (simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration), then explain the picture it creates. Use the glossary to learn the names.

Structure and layout

"Why is the last paragraph only one sentence?"

1 mark

Think about the effect on the reader. Headings help you find things, a short paragraph adds emphasis, a question hooks you in, bullet points make information quick to read.

Grammar and punctuation

"Why is a colon used in line 4?"

1 mark

Know the job of each mark: a colon introduces a list or explanation, brackets and dashes add extra information, an ellipsis builds suspense.

Purpose, audience and text type

"Who is this text written for? How do you know?"

1 to 2 marks

Name the audience or purpose, then point to a feature that proves it: simple words for young readers, facts and figures to inform, "you" to persuade.

Writing section

Writing guide

The features examiners look for in each text type. Open a type to see its checklist, a plan and a model opening.

ReportPaper 1 · Non-fiction

Features

  • A clear title and subheadings
  • An opening sentence that sums up the topic
  • Facts, not opinions
  • Third person and mostly present tense
  • Formal, precise vocabulary

Plan

  • Introduction: what is the report about?
  • Two or three sections, each with a subheading
  • A short conclusion that sums up

Model openingSnow leopards are large wild cats that live in the high mountains of Central Asia. This report explains where they live, what they eat and why they are endangered.

Persuasive letter or speechPaper 1 · Non-fiction

Features

  • A clear opinion stated at the start
  • Three strong reasons, each in its own paragraph
  • Persuasive techniques: rhetorical questions, facts, "you" and "we"
  • Connectives: firstly, furthermore, therefore
  • A strong ending that tells the reader what to do

Letter layout

  • Dear Mr / Mrs + surname,
  • Say why you are writing in the first sentence
  • End with "Yours sincerely," if you used their name
  • End with "Yours faithfully," after "Dear Sir or Madam"

Model openingDear Mrs Patel, I am writing to ask you to give our school a longer lunch break. Did you know that children who play outside every day concentrate better in lessons?

News or magazine articlePaper 1 · Non-fiction

Features

  • A short, catchy headline
  • First paragraph answers who, what, where, when
  • Past tense and third person for news
  • A quotation from someone involved, in speech marks
  • Short paragraphs

Plan

  • Headline
  • The main facts in one or two sentences
  • More detail, in order of importance
  • A quotation
  • What will happen next

Model openingRooftop Garden Feeds Whole School. Pupils at Hillside Primary picked their first vegetables on Monday, six months after turning an empty roof into a garden. "We grew forty kilos of tomatoes," said Year 6 pupil Lina Haddad.

InstructionsPaper 1 · Non-fiction

Features

  • A title that says what you will make or do
  • A list of what you need
  • Numbered steps in order
  • Imperative (command) verbs at the start: mix, cut, place
  • Time words: first, next, after that, finally

Make it better

  • Add a helpful tip or warning
  • Use adverbs for precision: gently, carefully
  • Keep each step to one action

Model step3. Carefully pour the water into the jar until it is half full. Tip: do this over the sink in case it spills.

Balanced argumentPaper 1 · Non-fiction

Features

  • A title that is a question
  • Points for and points against, given fairly
  • Formal language and third person
  • Contrast connectives: however, on the other hand, whereas
  • Your own opinion only in the conclusion

Plan

  • Introduce the question
  • Paragraph of reasons for
  • Paragraph of reasons against
  • Conclusion: weigh them up and decide

Model openingShould children have homework? Many teachers believe that homework helps pupils to practise what they have learnt. However, others argue that children need time to rest and play after a long school day.

StoryPaper 2 · Fiction

Features

  • An opening that hooks the reader: action, dialogue or a question
  • A clear problem and how it is solved
  • Show feelings through actions, not just "she was sad"
  • Speech punctuated correctly, with a new line for each speaker
  • Varied sentence openings and lengths

Plan in five parts

  • Opening: who, where, when
  • Build-up: something starts to happen
  • Problem: things go wrong
  • Resolution: how it is fixed
  • Ending: how the character feels now

Model openingThe door creaked. Maya froze, her torch trembling in her hand. Someone, or something, was already inside the lighthouse.

Description of a setting or characterPaper 2 · Fiction

Features

  • Use the senses: see, hear, smell, touch, taste
  • Precise nouns and strong verbs
  • Similes and metaphors, used carefully
  • Move around the scene in order: near to far, or left to right

Avoid

  • Lists of adjectives: "the big, dark, scary, old house"
  • Starting every sentence with "The" or "There was"
  • Telling a whole new story when the task asks for one scene

Model openingThe market woke slowly. Shutters rattled upwards, and the smell of warm bread drifted between the stalls like a friendly ghost.

A model answer, with notes

Task: Write a story about a character who finds something unexpected. (Paper 2, Section B)

"Don't touch it!" hissed Omar.1 But Layla was already kneeling in the wet sand, brushing seaweed from the lid of the box.

It was no bigger than a loaf of bread. Its brass corners had turned green, and something inside it was ticking.2

"It's probably just an old clock," she said, although her fingers were trembling.3 She lifted the lid.

Silence.4 Then a tiny silver bird unfolded its wings, blinked at the two children, and began to sing.

Layla looked at her brother and grinned. The beach had always been boring. Not any more.5

Why it earns marks

  1. Opens with dialogue to hook the reader. The speech is punctuated correctly.
  2. Precise detail ("brass corners", "ticking") builds mystery without a list of adjectives.
  3. Shows the feeling through an action instead of writing "she was scared". A complex sentence with "although".
  4. A one-word sentence for effect. Different sentence lengths earn grammar marks.
  5. The ending links back to the start and shows how the character has changed.

Exam technique

Exam tips

Six habits that win marks. Press the speaker on any tip to hear it read aloud.

01

Find it in the text

For "find" and "give" questions, the answer is written in the text. Underline it, then copy only what you need.

02

Read between the lines

For "why" and "how do you know" questions, give your idea and back it up with a word or phrase from the text.

03

Check the marks

A question worth two marks needs two points, or one point and a piece of evidence.

04

Plan before you write

Spend five minutes planning. Decide on your purpose, your audience and three or four paragraph topics.

05

Share your time

The writing task is worth half the marks, so leave half the time for it. Move on to Section B after thirty minutes.

06

Check your punctuation

Leave time at the end to check capital letters, full stops, commas in lists, apostrophes and speech marks.
