The pattern

What every paper looks like

Every Checkpoint paper from 2024 to 2026 follows the same shape. Once you know it, nothing on the day will surprise you.

Paper 1

Non-fiction

1 hour 10 minutes50 marks

Section A · Reading · 35 minutes · 25 marks

  • One text, usually an article, travel writing or a book extract about a place, a craft or a piece of history
  • 10 to 12 questions, with lots of 1-mark answers
  • Often a list or fact file, then a 40 or 50-word summary

Section B · Writing · 35 minutes · 25 marks

  • Real tasks: a website article about a local landmark, a travel article, an article for a partner school, a description of a favourite object, a recount of a journey
  • The topic usually links to the reading text
Paper 2

Fiction

1 hour 10 minutes50 marks

Section A · Reading · 35 minutes · 25 marks

  • One extract from a modern novel, with a short introduction in italics
  • 8 to 11 questions on character, language, punctuation and structure
  • Ends with a 4-mark “Do you think…?” question

Section B · Writing · 35 minutes · 25 marks

  • Always a story, linked to the extract: someone learning a new skill, a night-time visit, a dream come true, a first day at work, an unexpected event on holiday

The writing is marked the same way in every paper: content 5, structure 7, grammar and punctuation 7, vocabulary 3, spelling 3. Anything of 20 words or fewer gets 0, and anything under 60 words can get no more than 7.

Our findings

What comes up every time

We sorted every reading question in the 11 recent papers by type. These are the skills to master first. Each one has a workout in the skills gym.

  • Punctuation for effect11 of 11 papers“Why is a colon used?” “Why are brackets used?” Colons, semicolons, dashes, brackets, inverted commas, hyphens and italics have all appeared. Many are tick-box questions.Punctuation detective →
  • Words and phrases in context11 of 11 papers“Give one word that means…”, “Give a three-word phrase that shows…”, “What does this phrase mean?”Word hunter →
  • Structure and cohesion11 of 11 papersWhy a flashback here? Why start mid-conversation? What does “this” link back to? Number the information in order. Match paragraphs to ideas.Put it in order → Link it back →
  • The 4-mark “Do you think…?” question10 of 11 papersTick yes or no, give two quotations, and explain each one. It is usually the single biggest question in Section A.The 4-mark question →
  • Sentences and grammar for effect10 of 11 papersShort and long sentences, a subordinate clause placed first, the passive voice, one-line paragraphs, repetition.Sentence effects →
  • Literary techniques8 of 11 papersSimile, metaphor, personification, sibilance and imagery: name it, find an example, or explain what it suggests.Technique spotter →
  • Summary3 of 5 Paper 1s, and every sample Paper 1A list, table or fact file first, then a summary of 40 or 50 words in full sentences.Summary squeezer →

The surprise: there are far more short, precise questions than long essays. A typical Section A has six to eight 1-mark questions. Speed and accuracy on those matter as much as the long answers.

From the mark schemes

Where students lose marks

The mark schemes show exactly what examiners accept. These are the habits that cost the most.

Copying more than you need

When the question says “give one word”, write one word. If you copy a whole sentence, the examiner can’t tell which word you chose.

Explanations that repeat the quotation

In the 4-mark question, each explanation must say how the quotation supports your answer, in your own words. Repeating the quotation earns nothing.

Quotations from the wrong lines

If the question says “Look at lines 46–55”, the quotation must come from there. Use the line or paragraph numbers.

Going over the word limit

A summary that goes over the limit can get only 1 of the 2 marks. A summary written as notes or a list can get 0. Write joined-up sentences and count your words.

Ticking the wrong number of boxes

“Tick one box” means one, and “tick two boxes” means two. If you tick extra boxes, the examiner can’t tell which answer you mean.

The wrong text type in the writing

If the task asks for an article and you write a story, the content mark is capped. Decode the form, audience and purpose first.
