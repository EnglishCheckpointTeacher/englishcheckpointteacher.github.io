Know what to expect

The papers

Each paper has a reading section and a writing section worth the same number of marks. The writing task is linked to the theme of the reading texts.

Paper 1

Non-fiction

1 hour 10 minutes50 marks

Section A · Reading · 25 marks

  • Non-fiction texts such as articles, speeches, travel writing, reviews, letters and autobiography
  • Questions on meaning, language, structure, viewpoint and purpose
  • Often a short summary in your own words

Section B · Writing · 25 marks

  • One non-fiction task, usually an article, a description or a recount. Recent papers asked for a travel article, a website article about a local landmark and a description of a favourite object
  • A given form and audience, linked to the theme of the reading text

Fiction

1 hour 10 minutes50 marks

Section A · Reading · 25 marks

Where the marks are

How it's marked

Cambridge examiners mark your papers against the curriculum strands. This is what they are looking for.

Reading skills

Every reading question targets one of these.

  • Explicit meaning: select and retrieve information accurately
  • Implicit meaning: infer and deduce, with evidence
  • Language: analyse word choice and literary and rhetorical techniques
  • Structure: explain how a text is shaped and sequenced
  • Grammar and punctuation: explain the effect of a sentence form or mark
  • Appreciation: purpose, audience, viewpoint, theme and context

Writing marks

Your writing gets a mark for each of five things. Typical marks out of 25:

  • Structure of your text7Deliberate paragraphing, cohesion, a shaped opening and ending
  • Grammar and punctuation7A range of sentence forms used for effect, accurate punctuation
  • Content, purpose and audience5A convincing voice, well-developed ideas, the right register
  • Vocabulary and language3Precise, ambitious words and techniques chosen for effect
  • Spelling3Accurate, including complex and less common words

What this means for you: fourteen of the twenty-five writing marks reward structure and sentence control. Plan your paragraphs before you start, and vary your sentences on purpose.

Reading section

Question types and how to answer them

The mark in brackets tells you how much to write. One mark needs one clear point. Three marks need a developed explanation.

Retrieve and explain

"Give two reasons why the writer moved abroad."

1 to 2 marks

Find the information and state it briefly. If the question says "in your own words", copying a whole sentence will not earn the mark.

Words in context

"Explain what 'meticulous' suggests about the character."

1 to 2 marks

Give the meaning, then go further: what does the word suggest about the person, place or mood? Connotations earn the second mark.

Inference

"What impression do you get of the narrator's father?"

2 marks

State the impression in a word or two (strict, distant, proud), then prove it with a short quotation and a "this suggests" explanation.

Language analysis

"How does the writer make the storm seem dangerous?"

2 to 4 marks

Use Point, Evidence, Explain. Name the technique if you can, quote briefly, and explain the effect on the reader in detail. See the method below.

Structure

"Why does the writer end with a short sentence?"

1 to 2 marks

Think about order and pace: what the opening sets up, where the focus shifts, and why the ending lands the way it does.

Grammar and punctuation for effect

"What is the effect of the dashes in line 12?"

1 mark

Name the job the feature does in this sentence: dashes add an aside, a colon sets up a reveal, a list of three builds emphasis, the passive hides who is responsible.

Viewpoint and purpose

"What is the writer's attitude to city life?"

2 marks

Decide the attitude in one word (critical, nostalgic, hopeful), then support it with a quotation. Watch for bias: loaded words show you which side the writer is on.

Summary

"Summarise the problems the explorers faced. Use up to 50 words."

2 to 3 marks

Underline only the points that answer the question. Write them in your own words, in full sentences, with no quotations, examples or opinions. Count your words.

Comparing texts

"Compare how the two writers present their journeys."

When two texts are set

Write about both texts in every paragraph. Use comparative connectives: similarly, whereas, in contrast, both writers.

The analysis method: Point, Evidence, Explain

Question: How does the writer make the storm seem dangerous? Text: "The wind clawed at the shutters while the sea hurled itself against the harbour wall."

The writer presents the storm as a violent, living attacker.1 The wind is personified through the verb "clawed",2 which suggests a wild animal trying to tear its way into the house,3 so the reader feels that the people inside are being hunted.

In the same way, the sea "hurled itself"2 at the wall. The reflexive verb makes the sea seem to choose to attack,4 which makes the storm feel deliberate and impossible to reason with.

Why it earns marks

  1. Point. A clear answer to the question in one sentence.
  2. Evidence. Short quotations of one to three words, built into the sentence.
  3. Explain. "Suggests" leads into the connotation and the effect on the reader.
  4. Zooms in on a single word and uses the correct term for it.
Writing section

Writing guide

Before you plan, work out four things from the task: the form, the audience, the purpose and the tone. Then open the text type you need.

Argument or speechPaper 1 · Persuade your audience

Features

  • A clear position from the first paragraph
  • Three or four developed reasons, strongest last
  • Rhetorical devices: direct address, rhetorical questions, the rule of three, facts and statistics, emotive language
  • A counter-argument that you then answer
  • A call to action in the final line

Plan

  • Hook and position
  • Reason 1 with an example
  • Reason 2 with evidence
  • "Some people say… however…"
  • Strongest reason, then call to action

Model openingImagine a school where no one ever went hungry before a maths test. That school could be ours, and it would cost less than you think.

DiscussionPaper 1 · Balanced views

Features

  • An impersonal, measured tone
  • Both sides developed fairly, with evidence
  • Hedging language: it could be argued, many believe, this may suggest
  • Contrast connectives: nevertheless, conversely, whereas
  • A reasoned conclusion that weighs the two sides

Plan

  • Introduce the issue and why it matters
  • The case for
  • The case against
  • Conclusion: your judgement, and why

Model openingShould mobile phones be allowed in classrooms? The question divides parents, teachers and students alike, and both sides raise serious points.

Article or reviewPaper 1 · Inform and engage

Features

  • A headline, and a first paragraph that hooks the reader
  • A lively, confident voice suited to the publication
  • Topic sentences that make each paragraph's point clear
  • Anecdote, expert opinion or statistics for support
  • For a review: a clear verdict and a recommendation

Register check

  • School magazine: friendly, direct, some humour
  • Newspaper: formal, third person, factual
  • Blog: first person, conversational but controlled

Model openingNobody Warned Me About the Silence. Three days into a week without my phone, the strangest thing was not what I missed. It was what I started to notice.

Formal letterPaper 1 · Formal writing

Features

  • Dear + title and surname, or Dear Sir or Madam
  • Purpose stated in the opening sentence
  • One clear point per paragraph
  • Formal vocabulary, no contractions
  • Yours sincerely (named) or Yours faithfully (unnamed)

Useful phrases

  • I am writing to express my concern about…
  • I would urge you to consider…
  • I look forward to your response.

Model openingDear Ms Rahman, I am writing on behalf of the Year 9 student council to propose a change to the school's recycling scheme.

NarrativePaper 2 · Story writing

Features

  • One main event, two or three characters, a short time span
  • A deliberate structure: try starting in the middle of the action, or using a flashback
  • Show, don't tell: actions, senses and dialogue reveal feelings
  • Sentence length that controls pace: short for tension, long for calm
  • An ending that resolves or deliberately leaves a question

Avoid

  • Too much plot: a whole adventure in one page
  • "It was all a dream" endings
  • Long conversations that do not move the story on

Model openingThe letter had been sitting on the kitchen table for three days before Amira found the courage to open it.

Descriptive writingPaper 2 · Create atmosphere

Features

  • A single place or moment, explored in depth
  • A structure: wide shot, then zoom in, then a shift in time or mood
  • Imagery: simile, metaphor, personification, sound effects
  • All the senses, not just sight
  • A consistent mood created through word choice

Avoid

  • Turning it into a story
  • Piling up adjectives
  • Clichés such as "as cold as ice"

Model openingBy dusk the harbour had emptied. Ropes slapped lazily against the masts, and the last gull circled once, twice, then gave up and settled on the water.

A model answer, with notes

Task: Write a speech for your year group arguing that the school day should start later. (Paper 1, Section B)

Hands up if you hit the snooze button this morning.1 Keep them up if you hit it twice. Look around: that is most of us, and it is not because we are lazy.

Scientists who study sleep have shown that teenage brains do not fully wake until after nine o'clock.2 Yet here we are at eight, expected to solve equations while our bodies insist it is still night.

Some teachers will argue that a later start means a later finish. They are right. However,3 an hour of real learning is worth more than an hour of staring at a page.

We are not asking for less school. We are asking for better school. So sign the petition, speak to your form tutor, and let us start the day awake.4

Why it earns marks

  1. Direct address and an action for the audience: the form (a speech) is clear from the first line.
  2. Evidence gives the argument authority, in a formal register.
  3. A counter-argument is raised fairly, then answered. "However" marks the turn.
  4. Repetition, a rule of three and a call to action make a shaped ending.

Exam technique

Exam tips

Six habits that win marks. Press the speaker on any tip to hear it read aloud.

01

Point, Evidence, Explain

Make your point, quote a short piece of evidence, then explain the effect of the writer's words on the reader.

02

Zoom in on words

Pick one powerful word from a quotation and explore what it suggests. Strong verbs and adjectives carry meaning.

03

Think about structure

Ask why the writer opens, shifts or ends the text the way they do. Look for short sentences, paragraph changes and changes of focus.

04

Let the marks guide you

One mark needs one clear point. Three marks need a developed answer with evidence. Don't write a paragraph for a single mark.

05

Argue with power

Use rhetorical questions, facts, statistics, direct address and a counter-argument that you then knock down.

06

Show, don't tell

In narrative writing, show feelings through actions and senses. Vary your sentence lengths and openings to control the pace.

Four weeks to go

Revision plan and checklist

Short, regular sessions work best. Tick things off as you go. This device remembers your ticks.

  1. Week 1

    Reading analysis

    Learn the techniques toolkit. Write three Point, Evidence, Explain paragraphs on any article or story you are reading.

  2. Week 2

    Non-fiction writing

    Plan a speech, a discussion, an article and a formal letter. Write one in full in thirty minutes.

  3. Week 3

    Fiction writing

    Write one narrative and one description. Experiment with a flashback and with sentence length for pace.

  4. Week 4

    Timed practice

    Sit one full paper of each kind in 1 hour 10 minutes. Practise a fifty-word summary, and take the quiz until you score 9 or more.
